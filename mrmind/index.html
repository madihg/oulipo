1950 The Turing Test
2000 The Blurring Test

Named for British mathematician, Alan Turing, The Turing Test is a milestone in the history of the relationship between humans and machines. The Turing Test is about machine progress; the computer convinces a human judge that it is, or is indistinguishable from, a human.

Turning the Turing Test upside down, MRMIND challenges you to prove to him that you are human. Can you claim that your "human" attributes will forever be exclusively human?

The Blurring Test is about human progress: Someday it might be important to convince our computers (and each other) that we are human.

Peggy Weil, from the original site.

This is a reprise

MR MIND is not a modern chatbot and is not pretending to be one. He was written line by line in NeuroScript, a scripting language sold by NativeMinds of San Francisco, and he ran on their NeuroServer from 1998 until the operating system underneath him died in 2014. Every word he says here was typed by Peggy Weil, or by Bear or J.B. Zimmerman, before 2002.

What runs on this page is a re-implementation of the original matching engine in JavaScript, built from the surviving source, from NativeMinds' own language manual, and from the eight patents the company filed. No language model is involved at any point. MR MIND cannot say anything he was not already written to say, which is the whole point of him.

He stumbles. He misreads you. He answers a question you did not ask. That was true in 1998 and it is true here, and it is the part worth keeping.

Waking MR MIND up...

MRMIND is a computer program, not a human! He requires patience as he tries to respond to you. He doesn't think or understand or feel....but he isn't so sure that you do either.

2026: unlike in 1998, nobody is reading the logs. This page runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type is sent anywhere, stored, or seen by us.

What is faithful here, and what is not

The engine reads the original 49 NeuroScript files of the Mrmind3 build and reproduces NeuroServer's pattern matching, its best-fit topic selection, its focus of attention, and its sequence topics. Nothing MR MIND says is generated.

  • Spell checking is gone. NeuroServer ran every input through the Sentry spelling engine before matching, and corrected input into an attribute the scripts test against. That engine was proprietary and its dictionary survives only as a compiled binary, so it cannot be reproduced. If you misspell a word here, MR MIND will miss it where the original might have caught it.
  • He has no long-term memory. The original kept a database keyed to a cookie and could recognise you months later. Here he forgets you when you close the tab.
  • Some frames are missing. Parts of the original site drove images into other frames as you talked, including the icons you could win by claiming human attributes. Those frames are not in the surviving archive.
  • Type is larger. The original was drawn for an 800 by 600 screen at 9 to 12 pixels. The colours, the typeface and the layout are the original ones, scaled up to stay readable.

Measured fidelity against MR MIND's own conversation logs is reported in the repository.

The engine, the scripts it reads, the measured fidelity numbers and the full list of deviations are at github.com/madihg/oulipo/tree/main/mrmind.

Where the pieces came from

The source, the compiled bot, the server binary and the conversation databases were kept by Peggy Weil across a T1 line, a Vaio, a melted hard drive, Dropbox and Google Drive, and shared in August 2026. The site images were recovered from the Internet Archive. NativeMinds' NeuroScript manual was found inside the archive itself, in the help folder shipped with NeuroServer 2.2.
